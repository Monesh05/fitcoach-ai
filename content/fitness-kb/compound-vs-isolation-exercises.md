---
title: Compound vs. Isolation Exercises
category: training-principles
---

**Compound exercises** work multiple joints and muscle groups at once (e.g. squat, deadlift, bench press, overhead press, rows, pull-ups, lunges). **Isolation exercises** work a single joint and primarily one muscle group (e.g. bicep curls, leg extensions, lateral raises, tricep pushdowns).

Why compounds are usually the foundation of a program:
- They allow the most total weight to be moved, providing a strong overall strength and hypertrophy stimulus efficiently.
- They train movement patterns that transfer to daily life and sport (squatting, hinging, pushing, pulling, carrying).
- They're time-efficient — one exercise stimulates many muscles at once.

Why isolation exercises still have a place:
- They can target a specific muscle or weak point that compounds under-stimulate (e.g. direct bicep/tricep work, rear delts, calves).
- They allow training a muscle with less systemic fatigue than a heavy compound lift, useful when joints or the nervous system need a break but a muscle can still be trained.
- They're useful for correcting imbalances or working around an injury that limits a specific compound movement.

Practical program structure: most well-rounded programs build around 4-6 compound movements per week (e.g. a squat variation, a hinge/deadlift variation, a horizontal push, a vertical or horizontal pull, an overhead push) and then add 2-6 isolation exercises per session to address specific muscles, weak points, or aesthetic goals. Beginners can often progress on compounds alone; intermediate/advanced lifters typically benefit from adding more isolation work as compound-only progress slows.
