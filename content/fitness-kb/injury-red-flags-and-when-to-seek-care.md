---
title: Injury Red Flags — When to Modify Training vs. Seek Medical Care
category: safety
---

Most training discomfort is normal muscular fatigue or DOMS (see soreness guidance) and doesn't require stopping training — but certain signs warrant caution, modification, or professional evaluation rather than "pushing through."

**Signs that generally mean: stop the specific movement and modify training, monitor closely:**
- Sharp, sudden, localized pain during a specific movement (as opposed to a dull, generalized ache).
- Pain that is worse on one side than the other, or clearly joint-related rather than muscle-related.
- Pain that changes the movement pattern (e.g. noticeable limping, favoring one side, inability to complete a normal range of motion).
- A "pop" or sudden give sensation during a lift, especially if followed by swelling or instability.

**Signs that warrant prompt medical evaluation:**
- Significant swelling, bruising, or visible deformity around a joint.
- Pain and weakness that doesn't improve, or worsens, over several days of rest.
- Numbness, tingling, or radiating pain (e.g. down an arm or leg), which can indicate nerve involvement.
- Chest pain, unusual shortness of breath, dizziness, or irregular heartbeat during exercise — these are cardiovascular warning signs and require urgent medical attention, not just training modification.
- Dark, tea-colored urine after an unusually intense or unaccustomed workout, along with severe muscle pain/swelling — a rare but serious sign of rhabdomyolysis.

**General practice:**
- When in doubt, especially for joint pain that doesn't resolve within a few days or that recurs with a specific movement, working with a physical therapist or sports medicine physician is more effective than guessing at self-treatment, since they can diagnose the specific structure involved.
- "Working around" an injury (training unaffected muscle groups/movements while an injury heals) is usually reasonable once a medical professional has cleared it, rather than complete inactivity, which has its own downsides (deconditioning, mood impact).

This guidance is general safety information, not a diagnosis — a chatbot cannot examine or diagnose an injury, and persistent or serious symptoms should always be evaluated by a qualified medical professional.
