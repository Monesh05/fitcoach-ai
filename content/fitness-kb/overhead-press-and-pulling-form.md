---
title: Overhead Press and Pulling Exercise Form Checklist
category: exercise-form
---

**Standing overhead (barbell/dumbbell) press** — primary vertical push for shoulders and triceps:
- Start with the bar/dumbbells at shoulder height, core braced, glutes squeezed to avoid excessive lower-back arching.
- Press straight up, moving the head slightly back and then through ("through the window") once the bar clears the face, finishing with the bar over the mid-foot and biceps near the ears.
- Avoid excessive leaning back (a slight lean is normal to clear the face, but a large backward lean substitutes an incline-press-like motion and stresses the lower back).
- Common fault: pressing the bar forward in an arc away from the body instead of straight up — cue "press up and slightly back, not out."

**Pull-ups / lat pulldowns** — vertical pull for the back and biceps:
- Start from a full hang (pull-up) or fully extended arms (pulldown); avoid only doing small partial reps.
- Initiate by pulling the shoulder blades down and back before bending the elbows, to properly engage the lats rather than just the arms.
- Pull until the chin clears the bar (pull-up) or the bar reaches the upper chest (pulldown), without excessive body swinging (kipping) unless that specific style is intentional.
- Common fault: pulling primarily with the arms/shoulders shrugged up toward the ears — cue "lead with the elbows, pull shoulder blades down first."

**Barbell/dumbbell rows** — horizontal pull for the mid-back:
- Hinge at the hips with a flat back, similar starting position to a deadlift but typically lighter load and higher reps.
- Pull the weight toward the lower ribs/upper abdomen, driving the elbows back, squeezing the shoulder blades together at the top.
- Common fault: using excessive body momentum (jerking the torso up) to move the weight — cue "control the weight, minimize torso swing," reduce load if form breaks down.

For all pulling and pressing exercises, a full, controlled range of motion with good scapular (shoulder blade) control is more valuable for long-term shoulder health and muscle development than lifting the heaviest possible weight with poor technique.
