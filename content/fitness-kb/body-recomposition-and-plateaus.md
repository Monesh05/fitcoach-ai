---
title: Body Recomposition and Breaking Through Plateaus
category: training-principles
---

**Body recomposition** (building muscle and losing fat simultaneously) is possible but happens under narrower conditions than a dedicated bulk or cut:
- Most achievable for beginners (first 6-12 months of proper training), people returning to training after a long layoff ("muscle memory" from previously trained muscle), and people who are overweight with a higher fat percentage to draw energy from.
- For lean, experienced/advanced lifters, simultaneous muscle gain and fat loss is much slower and harder — a dedicated slight surplus (for gaining) or slight deficit (for losing), cycled over time, is generally more efficient.
- Recomposition still requires a solid protein intake (often the higher end, ~2.0-2.4 g/kg) and consistent progressive resistance training — calories are typically set at roughly maintenance or a very slight deficit.

**Common causes of a plateau (progress stalling) and how to address them:**
1. **Insufficient progressive overload** — training the same weights/reps for weeks without any planned increase. Fix: ensure a clear progression scheme (adding weight, reps, or sets over time) is actually being followed and tracked.
2. **Calorie tracking drift** — for fat loss/muscle gain plateaus, actual intake often creeps away from the intended target over time (portion sizes increasing, forgetting to log items). Fix: re-audit tracking accuracy for 1-2 weeks.
3. **Inadequate recovery** — poor sleep, high life stress, or insufficient rest between sessions can blunt adaptation even with a good training program. Fix: prioritize sleep and consider a deload week.
4. **Program has run its course** — for intermediate/advanced lifters, months on the same program without variation in volume/intensity can lead to stagnation. Fix: introduce planned periodization (see periodization guidance) or change exercise variations while keeping the same movement patterns.
5. **Realistic rate of natural progress** — especially for muscle gain, the rate of possible progress slows drastically after the first 1-2 years of consistent training; a "plateau" may simply reflect a more realistic (slower) natural rate of gain rather than something to fix aggressively.

Before making large changes, it's worth confirming a plateau is real (2-4+ weeks of genuinely stalled progress with consistent tracking) rather than normal week-to-week fluctuation.
