---
title: Cardio for Fat Loss and Cardiovascular Health
category: cardio
---

Cardiovascular exercise contributes to overall calorie expenditure and, independent of weight, provides substantial health benefits (heart health, mood, endurance, longevity). It is a useful tool for fat loss but is not strictly required — a calorie deficit through diet alone can also produce fat loss.

**Steady-state cardio** (continuous, moderate-intensity effort, e.g. jogging, cycling, incline walking):
- Sustainable for longer durations, generally easier to recover from than high-intensity work, and can be done frequently without significantly interfering with strength training recovery.
- A common practical target for general health is roughly 150 minutes/week of moderate-intensity cardio (e.g. 30 minutes, 5x/week), per widely cited public health guidelines.

**High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT)**:
- Alternates short bursts of near-maximal effort with recovery periods (e.g. 30 seconds sprint, 90 seconds walk, repeated).
- Time-efficient and can improve cardiovascular fitness in less total time than steady-state, but is more fatiguing and harder to recover from — 1-3 sessions/week is a common practical ceiling, especially alongside resistance training.
- Not inherently superior to steady-state for fat loss when total calorie expenditure is matched; the main advantage is time efficiency, not a special "afterburn" effect large enough to be a primary fat-loss strategy on its own.

**Balancing cardio with strength training goals:**
- Doing substantial cardio (especially high-intensity or long-duration) on the same day as heavy lifting, or in very high weekly volumes, can interfere with strength/muscle gains if recovery capacity is exceeded — this is sometimes called the "interference effect."
- For someone prioritizing muscle/strength, keeping cardio moderate (e.g. 2-4 sessions/week, low-to-moderate intensity) and separating hard cardio sessions from heavy lifting sessions by several hours (or different days) reduces this interference.
- For someone prioritizing fat loss with muscle retention as secondary, a bit more cardio volume is reasonable, but resistance training should still be prioritized to preserve muscle mass during a deficit.

Walking (particularly incline walking) is an underrated, low-fatigue-cost way to add calorie expenditure without meaningfully interfering with strength training recovery, and is often recommended as active recovery on rest days.
