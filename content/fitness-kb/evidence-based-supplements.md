---
title: Evidence-Based Supplements for Training
category: nutrition
---

Supplements are, by definition, secondary to a solid foundation of total calories, protein, training consistency, and sleep — no supplement compensates for gaps in those fundamentals. That said, a small number of supplements have strong evidence behind them:

**Strong evidence:**
- **Creatine monohydrate**: one of the most researched supplements in sports nutrition; consistently shown to increase strength, power output, and muscle mass when combined with resistance training. Typical dose: 3-5g/day, taken any time of day, no "loading phase" required (though loading can saturate stores faster). Safe for long-term use in healthy adults at standard doses.
- **Caffeine**: improves strength, endurance, and perceived exertion during exercise. Typical effective dose: roughly 3-6 mg/kg bodyweight, taken 30-60 minutes pre-workout. Tolerance builds with regular use; avoid late-day use if it disrupts sleep (sleep matters more for progress than the caffeine boost).
- **Protein powder (whey, casein, or plant-based)**: not magic, but a convenient, well-absorbed way to hit daily protein targets when whole food is impractical. Equivalent to whole-food protein for muscle-building purposes at matched total protein intake.

**Moderate evidence / situational:**
- **Beta-alanine**: may modestly improve performance in high-intensity efforts lasting 1-4 minutes (e.g. higher-rep sets, HIIT); can cause a harmless tingling sensation (paresthesia) at typical doses.
- **Citrulline malate**: some evidence for modestly reduced fatigue and improved rep performance in resistance training.
- **Omega-3 fish oil**: useful if dietary fish/omega-3 intake is low; general health benefits are better established than direct performance/muscle-building effects.
- **Vitamin D**: worth supplementing if blood levels are low (common in people with limited sun exposure), since deficiency can impair strength and bone health.

**Weak or no evidence for muscle-building/fat-loss claims:** most "fat burners," BCAAs (when total daily protein is already adequate), and many proprietary "pump" or "test booster" blends lack strong evidence for meaningful, direct effects beyond caffeine or other known active ingredients they may contain.

General safety note: supplements are less tightly regulated than medications in many countries; choosing products that are third-party tested (e.g. NSF Certified for Sport, Informed-Sport) reduces the risk of contamination or mislabeled ingredients, which matters especially for competitive athletes subject to drug testing.
