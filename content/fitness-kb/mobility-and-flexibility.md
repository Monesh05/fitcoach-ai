---
title: Mobility and Flexibility for Training
category: recovery
---

**Mobility** refers to how well a joint can move through its full range of motion under control (a combination of flexibility, strength, and motor control). **Flexibility** more narrowly refers to how far a muscle can passively lengthen. Both support safer, more effective training, particularly for exercises requiring a large range of motion (deep squats, overhead pressing).

**Static stretching** (holding a stretch, e.g. 20-60 seconds):
- Best placed after training or on separate mobility-focused days, since some research suggests static stretching immediately before a max-effort lift can temporarily reduce force/power output.
- Effective for gradually improving passive range of motion over weeks of consistent practice (e.g. 3-5x/week).

**Dynamic stretching / mobility drills** (moving through a range of motion, e.g. leg swings, hip circles, arm circles, walking lunges with a twist):
- Better suited to pre-workout warm-ups — raises tissue temperature and rehearses the movement pattern without the potential force-output cost of static stretching.

**Common areas that limit lifting technique and are worth targeted mobility work:**
- Ankle dorsiflexion — limited ankle mobility often shows up as heels rising during a squat or an excessively forward-leaning torso; calf/ankle mobility drills and possibly a slight heel-elevated shoe can help.
- Hip mobility (flexion, external rotation) — affects squat depth and comfort; hip flexor stretches, 90/90 stretches, and goblet squat holds are common tools.
- Thoracic spine (upper back) mobility — affects overhead pressing position and bar path; foam rolling and thoracic extension drills can help.
- Shoulder mobility — affects overhead pressing and front-rack positions; band pull-aparts, wall slides, and dedicated shoulder mobility drills are common tools.

**Practical guidance:** 5-10 minutes of movement-specific dynamic mobility before training, plus occasional dedicated mobility sessions (or a few minutes of static stretching after training) targeting the areas above, is sufficient for most lifters — extensive daily stretching routines are not required unless a specific mobility limitation is actively restricting technique or causing discomfort.
