---
title: Muscle Soreness (DOMS) and Active Recovery
category: recovery
---

**Delayed Onset Muscle Soreness (DOMS)** is the soreness felt roughly 24-72 hours after unfamiliar or unusually intense exercise (a new exercise, a big jump in volume/intensity, or a lot of eccentric/lengthening muscle action like downhill running or slow lowering phases).

Key facts about DOMS:
- DOMS is caused by microscopic muscle damage and the resulting inflammatory response, not lactic acid buildup (a common myth) — lactic acid clears from muscles within about an hour of exercise.
- DOMS is not a required indicator of a "good workout" — plenty of effective, productive training sessions produce little to no soreness once the body adapts to a given exercise or load, especially for experienced lifters.
- Soreness typically decreases with repeated exposure to the same exercise (the "repeated bout effect") even without changing weights, so a first-time exercise is often much sorer than the same exercise done again a week later.

Managing DOMS:
- Light activity (walking, easy cycling, gentle stretching) — "active recovery" — can modestly reduce the perception of soreness and support blood flow, though it doesn't dramatically speed the underlying repair process.
- Adequate protein and calories support muscle repair.
- Sleep is one of the most impactful recovery factors for reducing soreness and restoring performance.
- Foam rolling and massage may provide short-term relief of soreness/stiffness for some people, though effects are modest and temporary.

When soreness might indicate a problem rather than normal DOMS:
- Sharp, localized joint pain rather than diffuse muscle ache.
- Soreness lasting significantly longer than the usual 2-3 days, or accompanied by swelling, unusual weakness, or dark urine (a rare sign of rhabdomyolysis after extreme, unaccustomed exertion) — these warrant medical attention rather than just more rest.

Training through mild, normal DOMS at reduced intensity is generally fine and often recommended over complete rest, but pushing through sharp or joint-related pain is not.
